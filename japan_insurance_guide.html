1What insurance really is

Before anything Japanese, the one idea everything else hangs on.

Picture 1,000 people. Each year, roughly one of them will have a ¥10,000,000 disaster. Nobody knows who. If everyone puts ¥10,000 into a pot, the pot holds ¥10,000,000, which is exactly enough to pay whoever is unlucky. That's the whole trick. Insurance turns a large, uncertain loss into a small, certain payment.

Each dot pays a small premium into the pool. One red dot has the loss and gets paid from the pool. Real premiums are a bit higher than the expected loss to cover costs, including the seller's commission. That's chapter 4.

Two numbers drive everything: how likely the bad event is, and how much it costs. Multiply them and you get the expected loss, the fair price of the risk. An insurer then adds money for running the business, paying sellers and making a profit. That added slice is where every yen of commission in this guide comes from.

premium = probability × size of loss + loading (costs, commission, profit)

2The shape of Japan's market

Japan carves insurance into three "fields," keeps life and non-life in separate companies, and has a few players you won't find elsewhere.

The three fields

Japanese law sorts insurance into three bun'ya (fields):

Field 1 (life) pays a fixed amount when someone dies or survives to a date. Field 2 (non-life) reimburses an actual financial loss, like a crashed car or a burned house. Field 3 sits in between: medical, cancer, nursing-care and accident cover. Field 3 is huge in Japan. Aflac, for example, built a giant business on cancer insurance.

The wall between life and non-life

The rule that shapes everythingA single company may not write both life and non-life insurance. This is called seison-ho ken'ei kinshi (ban on doing both life and non-life). So groups run them as separate legal companies, for example Tokio Marine & Nichido Fire (non-life) and Tokio Marine & Nichido Life (life). Each has its own licence, its own agency contracts, its own books and its own commission statements.

For commission this matters a lot. An agency selling for both companies in a group gets two separate contracts and two separate statements, and the rules (rates, bonuses, clawbacks) differ completely between them.

Who the players are

Traditional big life insurers

Nippon Life, Dai-ichi Life, Meiji Yasuda, Sumitomo Life. Several are sōgo gaisha (mutual companies, owned by policyholders) rather than kabushiki gaisha (stock companies). Dai-ichi switched to a stock company in 2010. Their core channel is a large army of in-house saleswomen and salesmen.

Non-life megagroups

Three groups dominate: Tokio Marine, MS&AD (Mitsui Sumitomo and Aioi Nissay Dowa) and Sompo (Sompo Japan). They sell almost entirely through agencies, and car insurance is the biggest line.

Foreign-affiliated and newer life companies

Prudential, Gibraltar, MetLife, Aflac, AXA, plus domestic players like Sony Life and online insurers. Many use the "life planner" model: highly paid, commission-heavy professional sellers.

Japan Post Insurance (Kampo)

Sells simple savings-type life insurance through post offices. It had a major mis-selling scandal in 2019, which led to a long sales suspension.

Kyōsai (cooperatives)

kyōsai are mutual-aid schemes run by cooperatives like JA (agricultural co-op), Zenrosai and Coop. They look like insurance but are regulated under separate co-op laws, not the Insurance Business Act.

Small-amount, short-term insurers

shōgaku tanki hoken: cover up to one year (two for non-life) with small payouts, like pet insurance or tenant cover. They're registered, not licensed, so the regime is lighter.

4How a premium is built

Commission is a slice of the premium. To see the slice, cut the premium open.

Life: three guesses, three profits

A life premium is priced from three assumptions, the yotei kiso ritsu:

1. Assumed mortality

yotei shibō-ritsu: how many people of this age die each year.

2. Assumed interest

yotei riritsu: what the insurer expects to earn on premiums it holds. A higher assumed rate means a cheaper premium.

3. Assumed expenses

yotei jigyōhi-ritsu: running costs, including sales commission.

The three profits

If reality beats each guess, the insurer earns a mortality profit (shisa-eki), an interest profit (risa-eki) or an expense profit (hisa-eki). Together they're the san-rigen (three sources of profit).

The jun-hokenryō (pure premium) pays claims and builds reserves. The fuka-hokenryō (loading) pays for everything else. Commission lives inside the loading, which is why an insurer can't pay more than the loading allows over the policy's life. The proportions here are illustrative.

The insurer must hold a sekinin junbikin (policy reserve) for future payouts. Its overall safety is measured by the solvency margin hiritsu (solvency margin ratio). Below 200%, the FSA can step in. A new economic-value solvency regime (ESR) is replacing this from fiscal 2025.

IntuitionThe insurer spends a lot to win a policy in year one, mostly as commission, and earns it back from loading over many years. So an early lapse is a loss for the insurer. That one fact explains front-loaded commission, clawbacks and why persistency is rewarded.

Non-life: loss ratio and expense ratio

Non-life prices on a pure rate (expected claims) plus a loading. For car, fire and personal accident insurance, GIROJ publishes a sankō junritsu (reference pure rate) that insurers may use as a base. For compulsory car and earthquake insurance, it publishes the kijun ryōritsu (standard full rate) that everyone uses.

loss ratio + expense ratio = combined ratio, and under 100% means an underwriting profit

The songai-ritsu (loss ratio) is claims ÷ premiums. The jigyōhi-ritsu (expense ratio) includes the dairiten tesūryō (agency commission), which is the largest single expense. In non-life, the agency's own loss ratio feeds into its commission (chapter 8).

6Who sells insurance

Each channel has a different employer, legal status and pay model. Get the channel right and the pay plan follows.

1. In-house sales staff · eigyō shokuin

This is the classic channel for big life insurers: tens of thousands of employees, historically mostly women, sometimes called "Seiho Lady" (seiho redī). They visit homes and workplaces, often during lunch breaks at companies, which is shokuiki (workplace) sales. They're employees, paid through payroll. Their pay mixes fixed salary with performance pay, a guaranteed training salary for new recruits, and a rank system (chapter 8). They're organised into sales offices (eigyō-sho or kyoten) run by an office head (shochō), inside branches (shisha).

Foreign and newer insurers run a different in-house model, the life planner or life consultant. Hires are often career-switchers, pay is heavily commission-driven, and sales managers are paid on their unit's production.

2. Agencies · dairiten

An agency is a business that sells on behalf of insurers. Types:

  • Tied agency (senzoku dairiten): sells for one insurer only.
  • Multi-carrier agency (noriai dairiten): sells for several insurers. This includes walk-in insurance shops (raiten-gata hoken shoppu) in malls, independent advisers and large online comparison agencies. Since 2016 these have extra duties to explain how they compare and recommend products (hikaku suishō).
  • Non-life agencies split into professional agencies that only sell insurance (sengyō dairiten) and side-business agencies (fukugyō dairiten), such as car dealers, repair shops, real-estate agents and travel agents. Car dealers write a big share of car insurance. That's the background to the Bigmotor scandal.
  • Corporate in-house agencies (kigyō-kei dairiten): an agency owned by a large company that sells to the group's own employees and business.

Everyone who actually sells at an agency must be the agency's own employee (shiyōnin). The old practice of using contractors on commission-only agreements, inin-gata boshūnin, was phased out in the mid-2010s. The insurer pays the agency, never the agency's staff directly.

3. Bank sales · madohan

Banks and securities firms act as agencies. They mostly sell single-premium savings products: annuities, whole life for inheritance, and foreign-currency policies. Special rules apply (chapter 7), including commission disclosure and restrictions on pressuring borrowers.

4. Direct, online and brokers

Direct-writing insurers like online car insurers pay little or no commission. A broker (hoken nakadachinin) represents the customer, not the insurer. Brokers are rare in Japan and mostly serve corporate risks.

The 2016 duties

Understand the customer's wishes

ikō haaku gimu: find out what the customer wants, propose products that fit, and confirm the final product matches before they sign. It has to be recorded.

Give the information

jōhō teikyō gimu: explain the key points using the "contract summary" (keiyaku gaiyō) and "points of caution" document (chūi kanki jōhō).

Agency control systems

taisei seibi gimu: agencies must have systems for training, compliance and record-keeping. The bigger the agency, the heavier the duty.

Compare and recommend

hikaku suishō: a multi-carrier agency must explain why it recommends a given insurer's product, including when the reason is "we focus on this insurer," and record it.

Other conduct rules

  • Suitability (tekigōsei gensoku): don't sell a product that doesn't fit the customer's knowledge, assets and goals. It's strictest for "specified insurance contracts" (tokutei hoken keiyaku): variable, foreign-currency and market-linked products, where securities-style rules apply.
  • Elderly customers (kōreisha): industry guidelines call for extra care, such as a family member present, more than one meeting, and a second staff member confirming. Insurers typically apply this from age 70 or 75.
  • Banned practices under the Insurance Business Act include false explanations, hiding key facts, discouraging truthful health disclosure, rebating commission to the customer and unfair switching (fu-tō na norikae).
  • Bank sales protections (heigai bōshi sochi): banks may not use their lending power to push insurance, and must separate loan and insurance staff for business borrowers.
  • Commission disclosure: for foreign-currency and other single-premium savings products sold by banks, the commission rate is disclosed to customers. The FSA pushed this from the late 2010s.

Why the regulator cares about pay plans

Every scandal above had a pay-plan angle. Volume targets and high first-year commission reward churning (Japan Post). Agency points that reward sheer premium growth plus close insurer–dealer ties reward looking the other way (Bigmotor). So the FSA increasingly treats compensation design as a conduct issue. Pay should reward persistency, customer outcomes and compliance quality, not just volume.

8Compensation: the deep dive

Who gets paid, on what base, at what rate, when, and what can take it back.

8.1 Follow the money

The insurer's commission system pays three kinds of payee: its own employees (through payroll), agencies (through a statement plus consumption tax) and banks (also agencies, with a special pay shape). How an agency pays its own staff is outside the insurer's system.

8.2 Measuring production: what counts as "selling a lot"

8.4 Life agency commission

A life agency earns a rate × premium received. The rate depends on the product, the premium-paying term, the payment mode, the policy year and sometimes the insured's age. The big design choice is the shape over time:

Example schedule over policy years 1–10. Front-loaded pays a high year-1 rate, then small rates. Level pays the same each year. Both can be priced to roughly the same total over the policy's life, but front-loaded puts much more at risk if the policy lapses early. All rates are illustrative.

Year 1 is shonendo tesūryō (first-year commission). Later years are jinendo-ikō tesūryō or keizoku tesūryō (renewal commission), usually paid for a limited number of years. Some insurers let the agency pick a schedule. The FSA has pushed insurers to stop extreme front-loading, since it rewards churning.

Tier bonuses for life agencies (deep dive D3)

On top of the base rate, life insurers rank their agencies into tiers (dairiten ranku, shikaku) based on volume, persistency, number of qualified sellers and compliance. Higher tiers get a bonus rate (bōnasu tesūryō, shōrei-kin). Regulators now look closely at volume-based bonuses for multi-carrier agencies, because they can bias which insurer the agency "recommends."

8.5 Bank sales

Banks mostly sell single-premium products. The insurer pays one upfront commission, a % of the single premium. Foreign-currency and variable products historically carried the highest rates. Two special points:

  • The commission rate is disclosed to the customer, so every bank commission line needs a disclosed amount and rate that match what was paid.
  • Early surrender inside a set window reverses part of the commission, just like a lapse.

8.6 Non-life agency commission: the point system

This is the most Japan-specific piece. A non-life insurer pays an agency:

commission = premium × base rate × agency multiplier

The kihon tesūryō-ritsu (base rate) is set per product line. The multiplier comes from the agency's score in the dairiten tesūryō pointo seido (agency commission point system). The insurer scores each agency once a year on things like:

Design ruleThe multiplier used this year must come from last period's frozen score. If a late claim changes the live score, already-paid commission shouldn't move. Otherwise you get a loop where claims change points, points change rates and rates change commission.

8.7 Compulsory car insurance

jibaiseki has a publicly set premium, identical at every insurer, and a standardised commission level. It sits outside the point system. Never apply the multiplier to it.

8.8 Persistency

Deep dive with calculators: D5

keizoku-ritsu (persistency rate) is the share of policies still in force after N months. Japan's standard checkpoints are 13 months and 25 months, meaning the first and second annual payments were both made. Its mirror image is the early termination rate (sōki shōmetsu-ritsu). Persistency feeds in-house rank reviews, office bonuses, life agency tiers and non-life agency quality points. All of them should read one shared persistency table so they never disagree.

10Time, calendars and the monthly close

In Japan, a policy can be sold in one month and counted in the next.

  • Fiscal year (nendo): April to March. FY2026 runs from April 2026 to March 2027. It splits into kamiki (first half, April–September) and shimoki (second half, October–March).
  • Booking month (keijō-tsuki): the month a transaction counts in. It comes from the cut-off date (shime-bi), not the calendar month. With a cut-off on the 20th, a policy whose events happen on 21 May is counted in June.
  • Payment date (shiharai-bi): often around the 25th of the following month, moved earlier when it falls on a weekend or a Japanese public holiday.
  • Several dates per policy: application, disclosure, cover start, issue, booking month and payment date. Keep them all. Licences are checked on the application date, earning is tied to cover start, and money moves by booking month.
  • Trial runs (shisan): commission is calculated several times as a preview before the final run. Only the final run posts.
  • Closing (kessan, shime): once a month is closed and paid, it's frozen. Late events and rule fixes go into the next open month as adjustment lines.
  • Seasonality: sales peak at the fiscal-year end (March) and half-year end (September) because of rank reviews and contests. Expect volume spikes then.

11Tax on commissions

Premiums are tax-free, but commission isn't.

  • Premiums are exempt from consumption tax (shōhizei, currently 10%), so an insurer's sales are mostly tax-exempt.
  • Agency commission is taxable. It's a payment for a service, so the insurer pays commission plus 10% consumption tax.
  • Invoice system (inboisu seido, from October 2023): to deduct the tax it pays, a buyer needs a qualified invoice from a registered seller (tekikaku seikyūsho, with a "T" registration number). For agency commissions, the insurer usually prepares a shiire meisaisho (purchase statement) and the agency confirms it, instead of the agency writing its own invoice.
  • Unregistered agencies: the buyer may still deduct 80% of the tax for transactions through 30 September 2026, then 50% from 1 October 2026 to 30 September 2029. Key this to the date the commission was earned, not the payment date.
  • Keep it in proportion: because premiums are tax-exempt, an insurer can deduct only a small share of the tax it pays anyway. The unregistered-agency flag matters for correctness but barely moves totals.
  • Round once per statement: calculate tax on the statement total for each tax rate, not line by line, or rounding drifts by a few yen.
  • In-house staff are employees, so their pay isn't subject to consumption tax. It goes through payroll with income tax withholding and social insurance.

D1ANP: annualised premium

nenkansan hokenryō: the yardstick that makes ¥5,000 a month and ¥3,000,000 once comparable.

Why it exists

Suppose a seller writes a ¥10,000 monthly policy and a ¥1,200,000 single-premium policy. Which sold more? Cash collected this month says ¥1,200,000 dwarfs ¥10,000. But the monthly policy brings ¥120,000 every year for decades. ANP puts both on a "per year of cover" footing so new business can be compared, ranked and paid on.

Edge cases a system must handle

  • Riders (tokuyaku): ANP includes rider premiums, but riders can carry different weights and rates, so store them per component.
  • Conversions (tenkan): new ANP = new premium − old premium (only the increase). A conversion to a lower premium adds zero new business and reduces in-force.
  • Increases (zōgaku): an added amount on an existing policy counts as new ANP for the increase only.
  • Mode changes: switching from monthly to annual payment doesn't change ANP. It's the same yearly premium.
  • Cooling-off and early cancellations: reverse the ANP credit as well as the commission, or rank and bonus figures get inflated.
  • Foreign currency: ANP in yen at the insurer's applied rate on the booking date, locked so reports don't move with the exchange rate.

D2In-force growth

hoyū keiyaku: the book of live policies, and the real engine of insurer value and renewal commission.

Yes, it's a central concept. Japanese insurers track the in-force book (hoyū keiyaku) by number of policies, by sum insured (hoyū keiyaku-daka) and by in-force ANP. New business only matters if it stays. A seller who writes a lot but loses it quickly adds nothing to the book. That's why in-force growth, not new sales, is what many insurers now highlight.

closing in-force = opening + new business + increases & conversion net − lapses & surrenders − maturities & deaths − reductions

The year's change is the net increase (jun-zō). If it's negative, the book is shrinking, which is the long-run situation for much of Japan's traditional in-house channel given the aging population.

  • Orphaned policies (koji keiyaku): when a seller leaves, their customers are reassigned. The new servicing seller may get maintenance credit but not the original first-year commission.

D3Tier bonuses for life agencies

dairiten ranku, bōnasu tesūryō: the extra rate a life insurer pays its better agencies.

How a tier system is built

  1. Evaluation period: usually the previous fiscal year (April–March), sometimes a half-year.
  2. Criteria: each one has a threshold per tier. Typical criteria:
    • ANP or weighted premium with this insurer
    • 13-month and 25-month persistency
    • Number of registered sellers, and how many hold advanced qualifications
    • Compliance or audit score from the insurer's agency audit (dairiten kansa)
    • Complaint rate and error rate on applications (fubi-ritsu)
  3. Tier = the lowest tier met across all criteria. Excelling on volume doesn't make up for poor persistency.
  4. Knock-out gates: a serious compliance finding, an FSA administrative action or a major complaint caps the agency at the base tier whatever its numbers.
  5. Freeze: the tier is fixed for the whole next period and stored as a snapshot.
  6. Bonus: usually a % uplift on base commission, sometimes a separate annual payment (shōrei-kin).

D4Rank evaluation for in-house sales staff

shikaku satei: every half-year, each seller's rank is reviewed, and rank sets their pay table.

The mechanics

  • Periods: first half (April–September) and second half (October–March). Some companies review annually.
  • Criteria: weighted premium in the period (often the main one), policy count, persistency, industry qualifications (general, advanced, applied or university course), and sometimes in-force maintenance activity or customer satisfaction.
  • Promotion versus staying: moving up needs the full promotion threshold. Keeping a rank needs a lower bar, for example 80% of it. This gap stops sellers bouncing up and down each half-year.
  • One step at a time: a typical rule moves a seller at most one rank down per review, softening a single bad half-year.
  • Recruit protection: during the training period (ikusei kikan, often the first two years), the seller is on a guaranteed salary (ikusei-kyū) and isn't demoted, though minimum activity is still required. When protection ends there's a well-known cliff, and a lot of turnover happens then.
  • Special cases: maternity, childcare and sick leave usually freeze the review rather than count as a zero.
  • Effect: rank sets the performance-pay rate table, the bonus base and sometimes the fixed salary step.

D7Shared credit

kyōdō boshū (joint selling): one policy, several sellers, one set of rules for every downstream number.

What gets split

Once shares are set at the application, they should drive all of these the same way:

  • Production credit: ANP and weighted premium for rank, contests and tiers
  • First-year and renewal commission
  • Clawbacks: each seller gives back their own share of what they received
  • Persistency attribution: the cohort counts the policy for each seller, weighted by share or in full, depending on the plan

Rules that keep it clean

  • Shares must add up to exactly 100%, checked at entry and as a blocking data check.
  • Every co-seller must be licensed for the product on the application date. An unlicensed co-seller's share is held, not handed to the others.
  • Rounding: split in yen, round each share down, and give the leftover yen to the primary seller (shu-tantō). Then the parts always add up exactly to the whole.
  • Shares are fixed. Later servicing changes don't rewrite past splits.
  • Between agencies: when two agencies sell jointly, a lead agency (kanji dairiten) is named, and each agency gets its share on its own statement.
  • In-house trainer and recruit pairs often split 50/50, or 100% credit to the recruit plus a separate trainer allowance. These are two different designs, so pick one per plan.

D9Clawback, all the way down

reinyū: when, how much, from whom, and how the money actually comes back.

1. Paid as earned or paid in advance: the question that decides everything

Paid as earned

Commission is paid each month as each premium comes in. If the policy lapses in month 6, only 6 months of commission were ever paid, so the lapse is partly self-correcting. The clawback only needs to cover the higher first-year rate.

Paid in advance

The whole first-year commission (12 × monthly premium × rate) is paid at issue. If the policy lapses in month 6, half was paid for premiums that never arrived. That's unearned commission (mikeika tesūryō), and it has to come back.

  • Offset (sōsai): the clawback is netted against the payee's next statement. Normal and simple.

  • Demand for repayment (henkan seikyū): for agencies that end their contract or never earn enough to offset it.

M2.1 How riders work at MetLife Japan

A MetLife policy is built from a base contract (shu-keiyaku, 主契約) and optional riders (tokuyaku, 特約). The 2025 disclosure lists about 60 riders. The brochures and product pages show which ones attach to which product. Four rules from those documents shape how a policy, and its commission, is put together:

Rider or special provision

A tokuyaku (特約) adds cover and usually its own premium. A tokusoku (特則, special provision) changes how the base contract works. Examples are My Flexi's health support provision (kenkō sapōto tokusoku), which pays ¥50,000 for every 3 years without a short-hospitalisation claim, and the relaxed underwriting provision (hikiuke kijun kanwa tokusoku) attached to the My Flexi Gold versions of riders.

When a rider can be added

Some riders can only be added at issue, such as My Flexi's premium waiver for the three major diseases and Guard Next's cancer premium waiver. Others can be added mid-term (chūto fuka), but Guard Next riders can't be added in the first year. Each mid-term addition is a new component with its own start date.

Exclusions and limits

The eight-disease and three-disease extended-hospitalisation riders can't be held together. A customer can't hold MetLife advanced-medical riders on more than one policy. Some riders can't be cancelled while another is attached: on My Flexi, the rider that requires this can't be cancelled while a living-support or lifelong-death rider is attached.

Procedural riders

Many riders on foreign-currency and savings products add no cover. They control how money moves: paying premiums in yen or in foreign currency, paying claims in yen, converting a payout to an annuity, switching to yen, or taking periodic payouts. They carry no protection premium, but their exchange rates include a fee, and they decide which currency each event is booked in.

M3The distribution landscape

Four channels, as MetLife describes them itself, each run by its own division.

Channel sizes are from MetLife Japan's 2025 disclosure. The disclosure doesn't break new business down by channel. Bank sales lean toward single-premium foreign-currency products; consultants and agencies sell more protection, medical and corporate products.
Vocabulary trap: "Agency" at MetLifeMetLife's organisation chart has an Agency Division (eijenshī bumon, エイジェンシー部門) and a separate Agency Channel Division (dairiten bumon, 代理店部門). Following US usage, "agency" here means the career channel: MetLife's own employed consultants working out of "agency offices". Independent agencies (dairiten) sit in the other division. A data model that maps the word "agency" from MetLife source systems straight onto the guide's Agency entity would mix the two channels up.

1. Consultant employees (career channel)

About 4,100 consultant employees, managers included, work from 102 agency offices and sell to individuals and business owners. They are regular employees (seishain), not contractors, so their pay runs through payroll with withholding and social insurance (Ch. 11). Since 2015 MetLife has awarded a "Customer Centricity Meister" title to consultants who meet strict standards on customer reviews, long-term persistency and internal conduct. For FY2025, 380 of about 3,750 eligible consultants were certified. A title like this is a quality designation, not a sales rank, so in a data model it belongs with qualifications and evaluations, not pay rank.

2. Insurance agencies

About 5,800 agencies sell MetLife products. The disclosure lists their types:

  • Agencies that sell both life and non-life insurance (seison kengyō dairiten), often local non-life agencies
  • Captive corporate agencies (kikan dairiten) owned by a company group
  • Walk-in insurance shops (raitengata hoken dairiten)
  • Life-only agencies, either exclusive to MetLife or multi-insurer (sentoku / noriai)
  • Mail-order agencies (tsūshin hanbai dairiten)

Three structures shape this channel:

  • National Agency Federation (Zenkoku Dairitenkai Rengōkai), founded in 1992. Agency associations run in 48 districts, and the federation is independent of MetLife. In January 2025 it had 3,655 member agencies and 5,142 individual members. Its slogan is "coexistence and co-prosperity" (kyōzon kyōei).
  • Magnet Agency certification, introduced in 2018. It recognises agencies that meet standards for in-house training, sales process and sales quality. In a pay system, a status like this would be an effective-dated attribute of the agency contract, and could feed the quality side of commission.
  • ASR staff. MetLife employees who support agencies: they visit with agencies for policy servicing and proposals, and since 2022 they also make calls asking agencies to follow up with customers. This is the wholesaler role. In Japan, wholesalers are usually paid salary plus a bonus tied to their agencies' results, not commission.

3. Financial institutions

About 110 partners sell MetLife products: megabanks, trust banks, regional banks and securities firms. Bank sales (madohan) are mostly single-premium foreign-currency whole life and annuities, so this channel drives single-premium volume. MetLife provides banks with support materials to explain FX costs and risks, and "after-follow" guidelines and tools for servicing customers after the sale. The bank-sales protections from Ch. 7 apply in full: firewall rules, the cooling-off explanation, and commission disclosure.

4. Internet

Direct online applications started in 2011 for simple protection and medical products. There is no seller and no commission, but a pay system still needs this channel, so that direct sales aren't mistakenly credited to a seller.

M5How agencies are paid

MetLife publishes the principles of its agency commission system, though not the rates. Read them alongside the industry rules that now require publishing them.

What MetLife says about its agency commission system

MetLife's agency recruitment page describes its "agency remuneration system" (dairiten hōshū seido) as follows:

  1. The amount of commission varies with the level of service to customers and with sales volume.
  2. There are several types of commission, and every type has quality indicators (kuoriti shihyō) that measure service to customers.
  3. Agencies that don't meet MetLife's service standard are not paid the commission that depends on sales results and after-sale follow-up.
  4. There is no incentive pay weighted toward selling particular MetLife products.

The 2025 disclosure adds that MetLife treats agency commission as payment for the service the agency provides to customers, and commits to publishing its thinking on commission in plain terms. This is the quality-gated design described in D6: part of the commission is paid only if the agency passes a quality bar, and that bar works as a knock-out.

Neither MetLife nor the FSA has confirmed them, and they conflict with MetLife's published statement that it has no product-weighted incentives. They're included because they show exactly what regulators look for in agency commission design: tier thresholds that weight one product line over others.

What this means for pay design

  • Secondments and benefits are compensation risk. Seconded staff, sales support and other benefits given to banks and agencies are value transferred outside the commission schedule. A complete model tracks them per agency contract, so total cost per agency (commission plus benefits) can be compared with the published commission rules.
  • Tier formulas get audited. Every input that decides an agency's rank (D3) should be stored with its weight. Then "is this tier weighted toward one product?" can be answered from data.
  • Quality gates need evidence. If commission depends on quality indicators, the indicator values for each period must be frozen and auditable, like the agency ratings in the data model.
